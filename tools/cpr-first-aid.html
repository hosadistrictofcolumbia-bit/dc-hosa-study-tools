Read this before anything else. The 26-27 skills were rewritten to the AHA guidelines released February 2026, and the choking sheet is now identical whether your team works from AHA or Red Cross material. The two changes that catch returning competitors: choking is now 5 back blows alternating with 5 abdominal thrusts, not thrusts alone, and the 2-finger infant compression technique is gone — use the heel of one hand or 2-thumb encircling. If you learned this event last season, relearn those two.

The official 26-27 test plan

Only two topics, so the sub-bullets under each are what really structure the test. First Aid at 60 percent is thirty questions — twice the BLS block — and it is where teams that drill only compressions lose the round.

How ties are broken

Your final rank is the averaged Round One test score plus the Round Two skill score. If two teams tie on that total, the higher average test score wins — the written test is the tiebreaker for the medal, not just the gate into finals. Your team must also clear 70 percent on the combined skills to be recognized as a winner at ILC, however well you tested.

Round Two — Skills

13 approved skills from the 2026-2027 guideline, with the rated steps and their point values. One or more of these is drawn as your scenario.

How Round Two works. The top Round One scorers advance. You get a written scenario at the start of the round — the same one for everyone, and it is a secret topic you may not discuss until the event ends. Timing starts the moment you receive it. You must complete every step listed here, and if the equipment is not there, you say the step out loud instead. Judges stop you and withhold the point if a step risks harm or damages equipment. You need 70% or better across the combined skills to be recognized as a winner.
The judge marks what they can observe. Scoring happens against the rating sheet in real time, while you work. A step you perform correctly but silently can go unmarked, and a step you cannot physically perform still earns its point if you say it. So narrate continuously, and pair each step with its reason in a few words: “Checking the ID band against the requisition — wrong patient is the one error you can’t take back.” The reason is not padding. It is how a judge tells a competitor who memorized an order from one who understands it, and it is what gets you through a scenario that does not match anything you rehearsed.
If your scenario includes a patient, the judge holds the script. The patient is simulated, and the facts you need — breathing, pulse, skin colour, whether the bleeding stopped, what the medical bracelet says — come from the judge, reading from the scenario and the rating sheet. The guideline is explicit that judges answer questions about the patient’s condition only where the answer is on that script. So ask, out loud, and ask specifically: is there a pulse, is the bleeding controlled, what does the skin look like now. Information you do not request is information you do not get, and a judge who says nothing is not telling you everything is fine. Keep speaking to the patient regardless — introducing yourself, explaining what you are about to do, and obtaining consent are rated steps in their own right, and they are among the most commonly skipped.

Step wording and point values are reproduced from the official 2026-2027 rating sheets. Practice against them out loud, in order, on a timer.
