Read this before anything else. Three quarters of every Medical Terminology test is written from one book — Ehrlich's Medical Terminology for Health Professions. How ties are broken

New for 2026-2027: the tiebreaker is ten questions, down from more. It is not multiple choice. You are given a definition and you write the term — spelled correctly — from Taber's Cyclopedic Medical Dictionary. Recognition will not save you here; you have to produce the word cold. Medals are decided on this sheet, and most competitors never practice it.

Official References

These are the sources listed in the 2026-2027 guideline. Test items are written from them and nowhere else.

Ehrlich — Medical Terminology for Health Professions

Cengage Learning · latest edition (9e) · the primary source

Roughly three of every four test questions cite this book. Its word-part appendix — the prefix, suffix, and combining-form tables near the back — is the densest scoring territory in the entire event. If your school library has one copy of one book for this event, make it this one.

BookPublisher page — check edition before buying TopicMindTap companion — flashcards and audio pronunciation

Stanhope & Turnbull — Introduction to Medical Terminology

Goodheart-Willcox · latest edition (2e) · the secondary source

About one in six questions cites Stanhope. It carries body-structure and anatomy questions more often than word parts — planes, cavities, regions, and system overviews. G-W also sponsors this event and runs a HOSA webinar on it.

BookGoodheart-Willcox publisher page TopicHOSA Webinars — G-W Medical Terminology session

Taber's Cyclopedic Medical Dictionary

F.A. Davis · latest edition (23e) · the tiebreaker source

Taber's almost never appears on the multiple-choice test — but the ten written tiebreaker terms come straight out of it, cited by page. Practice by reading a definition and writing the term, spelled correctly. That is the exact task.

Free sources worth using

Not cited by HOSA — but they cost nothing and they work

If you cannot get the textbooks, these will carry you a long way. MedlinePlus is written by the National Library of Medicine and is the closest free equivalent to a clinical dictionary.
